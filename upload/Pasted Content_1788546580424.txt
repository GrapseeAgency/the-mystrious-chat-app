I went through the list as a **product-design and systems benchmark**, not merely as a catalogue of messaging features. The crucial question is not “what can users type here?” but:

> **What does this product turn conversation into?**

That distinction explains why Discord feels like a community operating system, Slack feels like a work operating system, Twitch feels like a live-media venue, Rec Room feels like a social game, Nextdoor feels like a neighbourhood network, and Figma feels like a design room rather than a chat application.

One important correction before the benchmark: your 100 names are **not actually 100 independent active chat platforms** in September 2026. Skype was retired on **5 May 2025**; Guilded was sunset at the end of 2025; Meta Workplace closed in May 2026; Amino shut down in December 2025. X Spaces is a feature of X, RCS is a messaging protocol, `bere.al` is associated with BeReal rather than being a separate network, Status Network is an Ethereum L2 rather than a messenger, and “Status IM”, “Session Messaging”, and “Twitter Spaces” overlap with other entries. ([Microsoft Support][1])

So I will **preserve all 100 positions**, but I will explicitly identify duplicates, protocols, dead products and uncertain names instead of inventing features.

# 1–20 — The major communication architectures

### 1. Discord — **the community operating system**

**Beyond chat:** Discord turns one conversation into a persistent world: servers contain categories, text channels, forum channels, voice channels, stages, roles, events and apps. The most important conceptual move is that **a community itself is the product**, not merely the messages inside it.

**What is unusually intelligent:** Apps can operate inside DMs, group chats, servers and voice calls; Activities allow games, quizzes and collaborative experiences to open directly inside the conversation. Developers can also expose commands through the launcher, slash commands or contextual actions on users/messages. ([Discord Apps][2])

**Design lesson:** Discord does not dump every capability into the composer. It distributes capabilities according to **context**: talk in a channel, gather in voice, organise through roles, perform actions through apps, play through Activities. That separation is one of its greatest UX achievements.

**Why it feels polished:** persistent server navigation + hierarchy + recognisable icons + restrained typography + immediate presence indicators make the interface feel like **a place you inhabit**, rather than a pile of messages.

---

### 2. Slack — **conversation → work**

Slack's killer idea is that conversation is only the front door. Channels organise people around projects or subjects, while Huddles, Canvas, Lists, files, workflows and integrations turn discussion into execution. ([Slack][3])

**The brilliant part:** a Huddle happens directly inside a channel or DM, can include video, multi-person screen sharing, a notes canvas and AI meeting notes. ([Slack][4])

Canvas is even more important: files, images, video, comments, threads, app data and workflows can live together instead of forcing users to leave Slack. ([Slack][5])

**Design lesson:** Slack's interface understands a crucial rule:

**Do not make the user decide which tool to use before they know what they are trying to accomplish.**

Conversation → canvas → workflow → action.

That is far more sophisticated than “chat + 50 buttons”.

---

### 3. WhatsApp — **messaging as universal infrastructure**

WhatsApp's strength is not feature extravagance. It is **frictionless ubiquity**.

The product has gradually expanded from private messaging into Communities, Channels, Status, business messaging, group calling, payments/business interactions in some markets and richer commerce.

Channels are particularly clever because they deliberately separate **broadcasting from conversation**. A Channel is not pretending to be a group chat: followers receive updates, react and vote without turning the feed into thousands of replies. ([WhatsApp Help Center][6])

**Design lesson:** WhatsApp demonstrates that the best information architecture often comes from asking:

> “Should this content be conversational, broadcast, private or transactional?”

Rather than treating everything as messages.

---

### 4. Telegram — **messenger → programmable ecosystem**

Telegram's defining leap is the **bot + Mini App architecture**. Developers can make bots perform practical tasks and Mini Apps can bring interactive services directly into Telegram; Mini Apps can generate media/files and even integrate with device-level shortcuts. ([Telegram][7])

Groups and channels can scale dramatically, while topics provide structured discussion inside communities.

**What makes it beyond chat:** the user can encounter a service without installing another application.

A payment flow, game, utility, store, form, bot, community or media experience can begin from a message.

**Design lesson:** Telegram shows the power of treating messages as **launch surfaces for applications**.

---

### 5. Signal — **private communication as a complete environment**

Signal has gone significantly beyond “encrypted SMS”. Current features include polls, pinned messages, group member labels, usernames/phone-number privacy, Stories, scheduled messages, screen sharing, chat folders, disappearing messages, themes and Note to Self. ([Signal Support][8])

The beautifully understated feature is **Note to Self**: your private messenger also becomes an encrypted personal notebook/file bridge. ([Signal Support][9])

Stories retain Signal's privacy philosophy rather than becoming an advertising feed; you control who can see them, including custom stories and group stories. ([Signal Support][10])

**Design lesson:** Signal's sophistication is mostly invisible. Its UX treats privacy as a **default interaction model**, not a settings page.

---

### 6. Element — **communication sovereignty**

Element is interesting because it is not merely a prettier secure messenger. It sits on Matrix and combines messaging, spaces, files, voice/video, integrations, widgets and self-hosting/decentralisation. ([Element][11])

Spaces group rooms into larger communities; Matrix itself can bridge communities across systems such as IRC, Discord and Slack. ([matrix.org][12])

**Beyond chat:** the deeper product is **ownership of communications infrastructure**.

**Design lesson:** Element demonstrates that “UX” can extend all the way down to architecture: a user can perceive decentralisation through spaces, homeservers, verification and interoperability rather than having to understand the protocol.

---

### 7. Revolt — **Discord-like community architecture with openness**

Revolt's appeal is its community-server architecture: servers, channels, roles, permissions and a Discord-like social structure, but with an open-source/community-oriented positioning.

**Beyond chat:** the meaningful differentiator is not a single killer feature; it is the ability to construct **your own communication environment** rather than merely participate in somebody else's feed.

**Design lesson:** Revolt reinforces how powerful Discord's information architecture is: server → categories → channels → permissions. The hierarchy creates mental order before conversation begins.

---

### 8. Beeper — **the inbox abstraction**

Beeper's innovation is conceptually enormous: it attempts to make many networks feel like **one messaging inbox**. Current Beeper connects services including WhatsApp, Instagram, Telegram, Signal, Messenger, X, Google Messages, Google Chat, LinkedIn, Discord and Slack. ([Beeper][13])

Its newer architecture emphasises on-device connections for many services, with cross-device synchronisation and productivity features such as reminders and scheduling. ([Beeper][13])

**Beyond chat:** Beeper's product is actually **information consolidation**.

**Design lesson:** it attacks one of modern communication's biggest problems: users do not have too few chats; they have **too many inboxes**.

---

### 9. Threema — **privacy-first communication with practical structure**

Threema's philosophy is closer to “secure communication toolkit” than social network. It combines messaging with encrypted calls, groups, file sharing, polls and organisational functionality.

**Beyond chat:** the differentiator is the attempt to make privacy compatible with normal daily collaboration rather than forcing the user into a minimalist cryptographic tool.

**Design lesson:** strong privacy UX works best when security mechanisms are integrated into ordinary interactions rather than presented as technical ceremony.

---

### 10. Viber — **messaging + expressive social layer + communities**

Viber combines private and group chats, voice/video calls, communities, stickers/GIFs, message editing/deletion, disappearing messages and synchronisation across devices. It also offers calls to ordinary phone numbers through Viber Out. ([Viber][14])

**Beyond chat:** Viber treats **expression** as a product category. Stickers, GIFs, reactions, custom content and communities are not afterthoughts.

**Design lesson:** communication is not only semantic information. It is emotion, identity, play and social signalling. Viber makes those layers visually available without replacing the basic message flow.

---

### 11. WeChat — **the super-app benchmark**

WeChat's real significance is what happens when messaging becomes a **gateway to an ecosystem**: social feeds, mini-programs, commerce, payments, services, official accounts and numerous everyday functions.

**Beyond chat:** the user does not think, “Now I am leaving messaging to use a service.” Messaging becomes the navigation layer for daily life.

**Design lesson:** the super-app model succeeds when the platform turns **identity + communication + transaction + service discovery** into one continuous experience.

---

### 12. LINE — **messenger as entertainment culture**

LINE combines conventional messaging with stickers, communities, social content, services, games and a deep sticker/character ecosystem.

**The clever part:** LINE has historically treated stickers as a **visual language**, not merely decoration.

A sticker communicates tone that text struggles to express: affection, sarcasm, celebration, embarrassment, frustration, etc.

**Design lesson:** a platform becomes culturally sticky when the UI acquires its own **native grammar of expression**.

---

### 13. KakaoTalk — **messaging + services + identity**

KakaoTalk is deeply integrated into the broader Kakao ecosystem: open chats, channels, commerce, gifts, business services and other products.

**Beyond chat:** communication becomes a gateway into an **identity-and-services ecosystem**.

**Design lesson:** “one app” works when adjacent capabilities share the same identity, contact graph and interaction vocabulary.

---

### 14. Session — **messaging without conventional identity assumptions**

Session is notable for privacy architecture based on decentralised communication and metadata protection, rather than simply applying encryption to a conventional phone-number messenger.

**Beyond chat:** the important product idea is **anonymous/pseudonymous identity**, where the user is not forced to expose a normal social identifier.

**Design lesson:** identity itself is a product decision. A messaging system can ask “Who are you?” very differently from WhatsApp or Signal.

---

### 15. Wickr — **secure organisational communication**

Wickr's legacy is particularly relevant to enterprise messaging: secure messaging, control, administration, retention and organisational deployment.

However, the old Wickr Pro product was discontinued and AWS consolidated the product line into AWS Wickr; AWS documentation confirms the migration/discontinuation path. ([Amazon Web Services][15])

**Beyond chat:** organisational policy becomes part of the communication product.

**Design lesson:** enterprise communication is not merely “consumer chat with more users”. Auditability, identity, administration and governance are first-class UX.

---

### 16. Matrix — **not a chat app; an entire communication protocol**

Matrix should be treated separately from Element.

Matrix is an open standard/ecosystem with servers, clients, bridges, bots, SDKs and decentralised communication. ([matrix.org][16])

Its most extraordinary property is federation: people on different servers can still communicate.

Bridges can connect Matrix environments with other communications systems such as Slack, Discord and IRC. ([matrix.org][17])

**Beyond chat:** Matrix is essentially an **internet protocol for conversations**.

**Design lesson:** the deepest level of product design is sometimes not an interface at all—it is the architecture that allows multiple interfaces to exist.

---

### 17. Twitch — **conversation attached to live media**

Twitch does not make chat the centre of gravity. **The stream is the event; chat is the audience layer.**

Subscriptions, Bits, emotes, raids, clips, moderation, channel points and live interaction transform viewers from passive spectators into participants.

**Why the interface works:** the stream occupies the dominant visual area; chat remains persistent but subordinate. This hierarchy mirrors what the user came to do.

**Design lesson:** UI hierarchy should reflect **the user's primary activity**, not the number of available features.

---

### 18. Microsoft Teams — **communication fused with the Microsoft work graph**

Teams brings chats and channels together with meetings, Loop components, files, Microsoft 365 content and third-party apps. ([Microsoft][18])

Collaborative notes can exist directly inside chats, with agendas, tasks and real-time editing; assigned tasks can flow into To Do/Planner. ([Microsoft Support][19])

**Beyond chat:** Teams turns communication into **work objects**.

**Design lesson:** the most powerful integration is not a link-out. It is when an object remains **alive inside the conversation**.

---

### 19. Google Chat — **conversation inside Google's information ecosystem**

Google Chat is strongest when it inherits the surrounding Google Workspace model: spaces, threaded conversations, files, Drive, Calendar, Meet, apps and collaborative documents.

**Beyond chat:** a message can be the doorway to a broader Workspace action.

**Design lesson:** Google's advantage is contextual continuity. The user already has identity, documents, calendar and organisational data, so communication becomes another layer over the same graph.

---

### 20. Zoom — **meeting platform evolving into work platform**

Zoom has been expanding from meetings into Team Chat, Docs, Whiteboards, Clips, AI Companion and Workspace-style information organisation.

The current AI chat panel can work from Zoom content such as meetings, chats and docs, while also accepting external data sources such as Google Drive and Microsoft 365. ([Zoom][20])

Zoom can also generate documents, data tables, whiteboards and clips through AI Companion. ([Zoom][21])

**Beyond chat:** Zoom is attempting to make **communication itself the source material for work**.

---

# 21–40 — collaboration, gaming and live communication

### 21. Webex — **meeting → workspace**

Webex organises work around Spaces and Teams where users can message, call, meet, share files and use whiteboards. ([Webex Help Center][22])

Its strength is continuity: a space remains after the meeting ends.

**Design lesson:** meeting software becomes much more useful when the room does not disappear after everyone hangs up.

---

### 22. Chanty — **chat with an action layer**

Chanty deliberately embeds tasks into communication: messages can become tasks, tasks have status/assignees, and task discussions remain contextual. It also offers Kanban, Teambook, announcements, calendars, polls, forms, calls and knowledge organisation. ([Chanty][23])

**Why the design is intelligent:** it does not make the user “copy the message into a project-management system”.

**The message itself becomes the work item.**

---

### 23. Flock — **team hub**

Flock combines channels and DMs with video calls, file discovery, app integrations, directories, announcements, mailing lists and bookmarks. Its interface explicitly positions itself as a place where users can perform several work actions without changing tabs. ([Flock][24])

**Design lesson:** one of the most underappreciated UX problems is **context switching**.

Flock attacks it directly.

---

### 24. Ryver — **forums + groups + direct communication**

Ryver separates public forums, private group conversations and person-to-person communication. ([Ryver][25])

**Beyond chat:** the useful distinction is between **broadcast/open discussion** and **private working conversation**.

**Design lesson:** conversation topology matters as much as messaging technology.

---

### 25. Mattermost — **self-hosted operational communication**

Mattermost is designed around channels, teams, discussions and direct messages, while extending into operational workflows, integrations and self-hosting.

**Beyond chat:** the product is particularly relevant to organisations that consider communications infrastructure part of operational control.

**Design lesson:** enterprise UX can be excellent without copying consumer platforms; **control and auditability can themselves be design features**.

---

### 26. Rocket.Chat — **chat + omnichannel service desk**

Rocket.Chat combines team collaboration with an omnichannel customer-engagement layer. Its documentation explicitly covers channels, teams, discussions, DMs, threads, file sharing, search, Livechat, email, WhatsApp and other channel integrations. ([Rocket.Chat Documentation][26])

**Beyond chat:** the same agent can handle an internal discussion and an external customer interaction.

**Design lesson:** the real object is not “message”; it is **conversation across channels**.

---

### 27. Zulip — **the topic architecture is the feature**

Zulip's defining innovation is topic-based conversation.

Several conversations can exist inside one channel without becoming one giant chronological river. Topics remain readable days later, and messages are automatically associated with the conversation being viewed. ([Zulip][27])

Zulip additionally has collaborative messages, polls, to-do lists, reminders, integrations, bots and even bridge functionality. ([Zulip][28])

**Why it is unusually intelligent:** it solves the fundamental weakness of traditional chat:

**time is a terrible information architecture.**

Topics turn chat from a river into an indexed knowledge system.

---

### 28. Fumble — **unverified entry**

I could not establish a sufficiently authoritative current product profile for a major communication platform called “Fumble” matching this benchmark.

That means I would **not invent a feature analysis** and pretend it is researched.

For a serious product benchmark, this position should be marked:

**Verification required — do not use as a feature source.**

---

### 29. Guilded — **historically superb gaming-community architecture, now closed**

Guilded was particularly interesting because it went beyond Discord-style messaging into gaming-specific structures: calendars, scheduling, forums, docs, voice, recruitment-style community organisation and server tooling.

But it is no longer an active platform: Roblox announced that Guilded would sunset at the end of 2025. ([DevForum][29])

**Design lesson from the product that existed:** community software becomes substantially more useful when it understands **what the community actually does**.

For gaming, that means events, teams, games, schedules and roles—not merely channels.

---

### 30. TeamSpeak — **voice architecture as the product**

TeamSpeak has historically concentrated on highly controllable voice servers, channels, permissions and low-level administration.

**Beyond chat:** it treats voice as infrastructure rather than simply “another message type”.

**Design lesson:** for some communities, the most important UX is not text—it is **presence, voice hierarchy, permissions and stability**.

---

### 31. Mumble — **low-latency positional communication**

Mumble's defining characteristic is high-quality low-latency voice, including positional audio in supported environments.

**Beyond chat:** users can perceive *where* someone is in relation to them in a virtual environment.

That is a profound shift from:

> “Who sent this message?”

to:

> “Where is that person in this space?”

---

### 32. Steam Chat — **social layer attached to gaming identity**

Steam Chat connects directly with game presence: friends can be grouped by game, favourites can be surfaced, group chats persist, and rich presence can show what friends are doing inside games. ([Steam Community][30])

**Beyond chat:** the system knows **what activity generated the social interaction**.

**Design lesson:** presence becomes more valuable when it represents something meaningful: “playing X”, “in a match”, “joinable”, rather than merely “online”.

---

### 33. Skype — **historic communication benchmark, no longer active**

Skype once revolutionised internet calling, screen sharing, group video, messaging and international calling.

But it was retired on **5 May 2025**, with Microsoft directing users towards Teams Free. ([Microsoft Support][1])

**Design lesson:** being historically dominant does not mean the information architecture remains optimal. Platforms can be displaced when a newer product absorbs their strongest capabilities into a broader ecosystem.

---

### 34. VRChat — **communication as inhabitable 3D space**

VRChat is radically different because identity is represented through avatars and conversation happens while users inhabit worlds.

**Beyond chat:** identity, body language, distance, spatial audio, gestures, world design and community events all become communication.

**Design lesson:** the strongest social interfaces sometimes remove the conventional UI almost entirely and let **the environment become the interface**.

---

### 35. Rec Room — **social network disguised as a game platform**

Rec Room describes itself as a social app played like a game, with millions of player-created rooms and a Maker Pen for constructing interactive experiences. ([Rec Room][31])

**Beyond chat:** the user can **build the thing they are talking about**.

That is an extraordinarily strong community loop:

Create → share → visit → play → discuss → remix.

---

### 36. Snapchat — **camera-first social communication**

Snapchat starts with the camera rather than the keyboard. Stories, Spotlight, Chat, Lenses and Maps create a system in which communication, entertainment and location-based discovery coexist. ([Snapchat][32])

**Why the design is exceptional:** each function has its own visual behaviour.

Lenses = transformation.
Stories = temporary narrative.
Chat = private conversation.
Spotlight = discovery.
Map = spatial awareness.

It avoids the mistake of making all content look like identical posts.

---

### 37. Instagram DMs — **private communication inside a visual identity network**

Instagram DMs inherit the platform's enormous media graph: profiles, posts, Reels, Stories and creators.

**Beyond chat:** sharing is contextual. You rarely send a naked URL—you send the Instagram object itself.

**Design lesson:** communication becomes richer when the network's **content objects are native to the conversation**.

---

### 38. TikTok Inbox — **social discovery feeding conversation**

TikTok's messaging is inseparable from video discovery.

The user can discover a piece of content, react to it, share it privately and bring other people into the context.

**Beyond chat:** the primary social object is **media**, while messaging is the connective tissue around it.

This reverses the classic messenger architecture.

---

### 39. X DMs — **private messaging attached to a public discourse network**

X DMs are interesting precisely because they exist beside public posting, Communities and Spaces.

**Beyond chat:** users can move between:

public statement → public conversation → live audio → private conversation.

X therefore treats messaging as one layer within a broader **public/private communication continuum**. X describes Communities, Spaces, DMs, long-form posts, video and subscription mechanisms as parts of one ecosystem. ([Help Center][33])

---

### 40. Threads — **conversation-first social network**

Threads is principally a public social-discussion environment rather than a traditional messenger.

Its relevant lesson is the distinction between **public conversation and private messaging**: the two have different incentives, visibility and interface needs.

**Design lesson:** never assume that a private DM UI can simply be scaled into a public community UI. The psychology changes.

---

# 41–60 — social identity, ephemeral communication and alternative protocols

### 41. iMessage — **communication as OS-level magic**

iMessage is powerful because communication is deeply integrated into Apple's ecosystem.

Features such as reactions, rich media, effects, shared experiences and safety/location-oriented functionality make the message thread feel like an interactive space rather than SMS.

**Design lesson:** platform-native communication feels luxurious when the underlying operating system removes friction.

The user does not feel like they are “opening another service”.

---

### 42. Google Messages — **RCS plus increasingly expressive messaging**

Google Messages combines SMS/MMS fallback with RCS capabilities such as typing indicators, read receipts, high-resolution media and richer group communication. ([Google Help][34])

Its current design experimentation also includes Screen Effects and custom conversation bubbles that let individual chats acquire distinct visual identities. ([Google Help][35])

**Design lesson:** personalisation can be valuable when it is **local to the relationship** rather than an endless global theme store.

---

### 43. RCS Chat — **not an app**

RCS is a communication protocol.

It provides capabilities such as typing indicators, read receipts, Wi-Fi/data messaging and high-resolution file/media transfer. ([Google Help][34])

The important “beyond chat” lesson is architectural:

RCS attempts to make **carrier messaging behave like modern internet messaging**.

It is therefore a protocol-level benchmark rather than a UI benchmark.

---

### 44. Voxer — **voice messaging as walkie-talkie communication**

Voxer is built around push-to-talk/live voice combined with conventional text and media.

**Beyond chat:** voice is neither a phone call nor a recorded voicemail; it sits in the middle as **asynchronous radio**.

**Design lesson:** communication modes do not need to be binary (“text” vs “call”). There is an enormous design space between them.

---

### 45. Zello — **professional push-to-talk**

Zello applies the walkie-talkie model to smartphones, with channels, push-to-talk communication and location-aware/team-oriented scenarios.

**Beyond chat:** the interaction is designed around **fast coordination**, not leisurely conversation.

**Design lesson:** optimise the UI around the temporal needs of the activity.

---

### 46. Marco Polo — **asynchronous video relationships**

Marco Polo's basic insight is beautifully simple: people can send video messages without requiring both parties to be available at the same time.

**Beyond chat:** it makes video **asynchronous social presence**.

Unlike live calls, the receiver can consume it when convenient.

---

### 47. Clubhouse — **conversation as a live room**

Clubhouse's important contribution is the idea that social audio should feel like entering rooms rather than opening a call.

Hosts, speakers, listeners, moderation and topic discovery create a **stage/listener hierarchy**.

**Design lesson:** audio becomes dramatically more understandable when the product gives the conversation a spatial/social structure.

---

### 48. Twitter Spaces — **live-audio layer of X**

This is not a separate platform.

X Spaces lets people join live audio conversations, request the ability to speak, and lets hosts manage speaker access; X also provides discoverability and captions. ([X Business][36])

**Its cleverness:** the conversation appears in the timeline, meaning the **social graph supplies the audience**.

---

### 49. BeReal — **time pressure as social architecture**

BeReal's core design historically revolved around a prompt-driven moment where users capture an unpolished glimpse of real life.

**Beyond chat:** the feature is not another message type; it establishes a **social ritual**.

That is important.

The most powerful social products often create rituals rather than simply adding functionality.

---

### 50. Bere.al — **BeReal domain, not independent platform**

This is not a separate messaging ecosystem.

`bere.al` is associated with BeReal infrastructure/web properties; it should therefore not be counted separately from BeReal. ([User Bradley][37])

---

### 51. Status — **messenger + wallet + decentralised communities**

Status is a particularly unusual super-app: private messaging, decentralised communities and a self-custodial crypto wallet exist in the same application. ([Status][38])

**Beyond chat:** users can communicate, hold assets, transact and interact with decentralised applications.

**Design lesson:** this is a powerful example of **identity + communication + ownership** being placed under one product.

---

### 52. Status IM — **same Status ecosystem**

“Status IM” should not be treated as a separate full platform from Status Messenger.

The distinctive architecture remains decentralised messaging, pseudonymous identity and community communication. ([Status][38])

---

### 53. Status Network — **not a messenger**

Status Network is an Ethereum Layer-2 network intended to support decentralised social applications, games and protocols. ([Status Network][39])

Its lesson is infrastructure-oriented:

**What if the social platform were financially and technically programmable at network level?**

That is different from normal chat software.

---

### 54. Session Messaging — **same Session ecosystem**

This repeats #14 conceptually.

Session's important contribution is metadata-resistant, decentralised/pseudonymous communication.

The design lesson is about **identity minimisation**, not another independent interface.

---

### 55. SimpleX Chat — **remove the social identifier itself**

SimpleX is one of the most intellectually interesting messaging systems on your list.

It uses temporary anonymous pairwise identifiers instead of conventional public profile identifiers, alongside encrypted messaging, groups, files, calls, disappearing messages, portable encrypted storage and incognito mode. ([SimpleX Chat][40])

**Beyond chat:** the product questions the assumption that every user needs a stable identity identifier.

**Design lesson:** sometimes the most innovative UX comes from removing an assumption rather than adding a button.

---

### 56. Briar — **communication when the internet disappears**

Briar is exceptional for resilience.

It can synchronise over Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, Tor and even removable storage; it also offers private messages, public forums and blogs. ([Briar][41])

**Beyond chat:** this is communication designed around **failure conditions**.

No central server.
No permanent assumption of connectivity.
No assumption that the internet is healthy.

**Design lesson:** resilience itself can be a user-facing product capability.

---

### 57. Jami — **distributed communications suite**

Jami provides messaging, audio/video calls, group chats, conferences, screen sharing, media streaming, recorded messages, an extension system and even SIP functionality. ([Jami][42])

**Beyond chat:** it is a peer-to-peer communications toolkit.

**Design lesson:** modularity becomes more meaningful when the underlying architecture actually supports new communication behaviours.

---

### 58. Tox — **peer-to-peer identity and communication**

Tox is a decentralised communications protocol/ecosystem designed around secure peer-to-peer messaging, voice/video and file transfer.

**Beyond chat:** again, the interesting product is the **network model**, rather than decorative UI.

---

### 59. Wire — **secure collaboration rather than secure texting**

Wire combines encrypted messaging, calls, files, conferencing and document collaboration; its collaboration layer keeps files associated with verified group membership. ([Wire][43])

**Beyond chat:** a secure conversation becomes a **secure collaborative workspace**.

**Design lesson:** privacy does not have to mean minimalism. Security can support rich interfaces.

---

### 60. Dust — **ephemerality as product philosophy**

Dust's interesting proposition is centred around temporary/disappearing communication and privacy-oriented interactions.

Its strongest design principle is therefore:

**communication does not necessarily have to become a permanent archive.**

That contrasts with Slack, Discord and email, whose history becomes increasingly valuable over time.

---

# 61–80 — enterprise communication, customer communication and the “work graph”

### 61. Keybase — **identity + cryptographic collaboration**

Keybase historically connected encrypted communication with cryptographic identity, teams and developer-oriented workflows such as secure file handling and Git integration.

**Beyond chat:** it treated identity as something mathematically verifiable rather than merely represented by a username.

**Design lesson:** trust can be a product layer.

---

### 62. Spike — **email transformed into conversational communication**

Spike's distinctive idea is to make email behave more like modern chat while adding collaborative notes, tasks and meetings.

**Beyond chat:** it attacks an enormous UX problem:

email contains conversations, tasks, documents and scheduling—but usually distributes them across separate conceptual layers.

Spike attempts to make the inbox feel like a **communication workspace**.

---

### 63. Missive — **shared inbox as collaborative conversation**

Missive treats customer/team email as something multiple teammates can work on together.

**Beyond chat:** internal comments, assignments, rules and automation happen around the external conversation without exposing internal discussion to the customer.

**Design lesson:** one of the most powerful collaboration patterns is:

**external message + private internal layer**.

---

### 64. Front — **operationalising the inbox**

Front's shared inbox model lets teams collaborate internally using comments while customers see only the external conversation. ([Front Help Center][44])

Its shared inboxes can combine channels including email, Instagram and SMS. ([Front Help Center][45])

Front even merges duplicate messages across shared inboxes by default so actions remain coordinated. ([Front Help Center][46])

**Beyond chat:** communication becomes **work allocation**.

---

### 65. Intercom — **customer conversation → customer operation**

Intercom is much more than live chat: its Inbox unifies email, live chat, phone, WhatsApp and social conversations, while AI Copilot can use past conversations and knowledge sources to support agents. ([Intercom][47])

Its 2026 Messenger also has a dedicated Tickets space, separating ongoing conversation from structured case management. ([Intercom][48])

**This is exceptionally intelligent UX:** conversation remains conversational until it becomes a case—and then the UI changes appropriately.

---

### 66. Drift — **conversation as sales routing**

Drift's traditional strength is conversational marketing: chat, qualification, routing and meeting scheduling.

**Beyond chat:** its purpose is not “talk to customers”; it is:

visitor → identify intent → qualify → route → book → convert.

**Design lesson:** the strongest business messaging products have a **goal hidden behind the conversation**.

---

### 67. Crisp — **shared customer communication hub**

Crisp combines customer messaging with shared team inboxes, automation, knowledge and support workflows.

**Beyond chat:** multiple agents can work around the same customer relationship rather than treating each message as an isolated ticket.

**Design lesson:** communication history is more useful when attached to **customer context**.

---

### 68. Tidio — **human support + automation**

Tidio combines live chat with AI/chatbot automation.

**Beyond chat:** a conversation can begin with automation and escalate to a human when necessary.

The important design concept is not the bot itself but **handoff continuity**.

A human should inherit the context rather than restarting the conversation.

---

### 69. Zendesk Chat — **communication as support infrastructure**

Zendesk's broader platform turns chat into a support system around tickets, knowledge, customer profiles, automation and omnichannel communication.

**Beyond chat:** the unit of work is not the message; it is the **customer problem**.

That distinction is crucial for any support-oriented platform.

---

### 70. HubSpot Chat — **conversation attached to CRM intelligence**

HubSpot connects live chat with CRM records, marketing, sales pipelines, forms and automation.

**Beyond chat:** communication contributes data to a larger customer lifecycle.

**Design lesson:** the chat interface becomes more useful when the system understands **who the person is and what has already happened**.

---

### 71. ManyChat — **message automation as a marketing programming language**

ManyChat turns social messaging into automations: triggers, flows, replies, campaigns, comment-triggered interactions and lead journeys.

**Beyond chat:** the user is effectively building a **conversation program**.

That is a completely different mental model from sending messages manually.

---

### 72. Chatfuel — **no-code conversational automation**

Chatfuel's core idea is similar: automate messaging flows across social/business channels.

**Beyond chat:** conversation becomes a **state machine**:

trigger → condition → response → branch → conversion.

**Design lesson:** conversational UI becomes powerful when it is designed not only for recipients but also for **builders**.

---

### 73. MobileMonkey — **social engagement automation**

MobileMonkey has been particularly notable for Instagram/Facebook messaging automation, including automatic responses to comments, mentions and incoming messages. ([MobileMonkey][49])

**Beyond chat:** a public action—comment, mention, interaction—can automatically open a private conversational workflow.

**Design lesson:** social and private communication can form a **funnel**, not two disconnected worlds.

---

### 74. Landbot — **conversation as interface**

Landbot lets organisations build conversational experiences and bots rather than ordinary forms.

**Beyond chat:** a conversation replaces a traditional form or workflow UI.

The clever part is progressive disclosure:

the interface only asks the next relevant question.

---

### 75. Tars — **conversational conversion journeys**

Tars uses chatbot-style interfaces for lead capture, qualification and conversion.

**Beyond chat:** the interaction is structured around outcomes—booking, qualification, conversion—not open-ended discussion.

**Design lesson:** “chat” is often a visual metaphor for a **guided workflow**.

---

### 76. Typeform Chat — **forms redesigned as conversations**

Typeform's conversational system is especially interesting because it applies chat principles to data collection.

Its AI can construct and refine forms and workflows, while its FAQ-with-AI question type lets respondents ask contextual questions during the interaction. ([Typeform Help Center][50])

Typeform also supports branching questions and rich media/video forms. ([Typeform][51])

**Beyond chat:** it converts the old form from:

Question 1 → Question 2 → Question 3

into:

**responsive dialogue → context → relevant next question.**

That is excellent information architecture.

---

### 77. Jostle — **people-centric intranet**

Jostle is a beautiful example of going beyond document-centric enterprise UX.

It centres people, organisation, news, knowledge, events, recognition and employee discovery. Its people views can locate colleagues by role, location or expertise; its knowledge system contains policies, forms and training; its AI can return context-aware answers. ([Jostle][52])

It also has visual organisational structures and recognition/Shout-Outs. ([Jostle][53])

**Design lesson:** Jostle asks a better question than “where are the documents?”

It asks:

> **Who is in this organisation, what do they know, and how do we help people find each other?**

---

### 78. Workplace by Meta — **closed, but historically important**

Workplace attempted to turn Facebook-like social interaction into an enterprise network.

It combined groups, announcements, profiles, social feeds, live video and organisational communication.

But Meta shut Workplace down in May 2026. ([Meta][54])

**Design lesson:** its biggest legacy is the recognition that company communication benefits from **social dynamics**, not only project-management structures.

---

### 79. Basecamp — **project as a home**

Basecamp's architecture is unusually disciplined.

Every project receives a dedicated home containing to-dos, message boards, chat rooms, calendars, Kanban-style Card Tables and other tools. ([Basecamp][55])

Its Message Boards separate durable discussions from ephemeral Campfire-style chat, while Hill Charts communicate **uncertainty and project state** rather than merely percentage completion. ([Basecamp][55])

**This is extremely smart design.**

Basecamp understands that:

> chat is transient; project knowledge should be durable.

---

### 80. ClickUp Chat — **conversation inside the work graph**

ClickUp's strength is not chat alone; it is that chat sits beside tasks, docs, whiteboards and projects.

Its Whiteboards can contain live tasks and Docs, and sticky notes/shapes can become tasks. ([ClickUp Help][56])

**Beyond chat:** ideas can become work without translation.

**Design lesson:** the best collaboration systems make the transformation:

**thought → discussion → artefact → task → outcome**

feel like one continuous interaction.

# 81–100 — knowledge, creative collaboration, spatial worlds and communities

### 81. Asana Chat — **conversation attached to accountability**

Asana's real strength is what happens after someone says something.

Its task/project system supports forms, dependencies, rules, approvals, goals, timelines, portfolios, workload and automation. ([Asana Help Center][57])

The Inbox lets users filter updates and turn notifications directly into follow-up tasks. ([Asana][58])

Its newer AI layer adds smart summaries, answers, status generation, rules, AI teammates and an AI “Chief of Staff”-style assistant. ([Asana Help Center][59])

**Beyond chat:** communication becomes **accountability**.

### 82. Monday Chat — **conversation connected to structured data**

monday.com's differentiator is its visual work-management layer.

The 2026 platform positioning has become explicitly AI-first, with agents capable of working across workflows, departments and live operational data. ([monday.com][60])

**Beyond chat:** conversation can become an instruction to an agent operating over structured organisational data.

That is the beginning of the transition from:

chatbot → **work agent**.

### 83. Notion Chat — **conversation + knowledge graph**

Notion's power comes from the fact that chat or comments can live beside Docs, databases, wikis, projects, meeting notes and other knowledge objects.

Its Docs support dozens of content types, including code, toggles, embedded media, charts and structured content, with comments and co-editing directly attached to the document. ([Notion][61])

**Beyond chat:** conversation can directly modify the organisation's **memory**.

**Design lesson:** the ultimate collaboration system may not be a messenger at all; it may be a **shared memory system with conversation wrapped around it**.

### 84. Miro Chat — **conversation embodied on a canvas**

Miro's strength is visual spatial collaboration.

Its AI Workflows allow teams to transform existing canvas context into structured deliverables, while AI agents/Sidekicks can operate within the canvas. 

**Beyond chat:** rather than saying “the answer is…” in a message, a participant can **draw the answer**.

That is a fundamentally different communication medium.

### 85. Figma Chat — **communication attached to the artefact**

Figma is one of the strongest examples on your entire list.

Multiple collaborators can edit a file simultaneously, leave comments directly on designs, use audio, cursor chat and Spotlight, inspect versions and collaborate in presentations. ([Figma][62])

**Beyond chat:** a conversation is anchored to **the exact object being discussed**.

Instead of:

That removes ambiguity.

### 86. Gather.town — **office as a game-like map**

### 87. Kumospace — **spatial video communication**

Kumospace uses virtual spaces and proximity-oriented video interaction to create more natural group gatherings.

**Beyond chat:** the user interacts with **distance and room layout**, not just participant lists.

**Design lesson:** hybrid-work interfaces are more believable when they reproduce some of the subtle social signals of physical space.

### 88. Topia — **virtual social worlds**

Topia applies spatial environments to gatherings, events and communities.

**Beyond chat:** the world is a social object.

The event can have rooms, proximity, movement, identities and experiences.

**Design lesson:** social UX can be designed as **environmental architecture** rather than merely screen architecture.

### 89. Spatial — **social interaction through immersive environments**

Spatial is focused heavily on immersive/social 3D environments, worlds and avatars.

**Beyond chat:** users communicate through objects, environment, avatars, movement and shared space.

### 90. Roblox Chat — **conversation inside an experience**

Roblox's communication system is interesting because chat happens inside user-created experiences.

**Beyond chat:** the conversation exists because the players are already **doing something together**.

That distinction is tremendously important.

### 91. Minecraft Chat — **communication attached to creation**

**Beyond chat:** shared environments become the conversation.

The world becomes a persistent message.

### 92. Among Us Chat — **communication under gameplay pressure**

### 94. Hago — **games + live social interaction**

### 95. Yubo — **friend discovery + live social interaction**

relationship maintenance,
relationship discovery,

Mixing all three into one feed often produces clutter.

# Now the really important part: **why do the best ones not look like scribble?**

This is where the 100-platform comparison becomes genuinely useful.

The products that feel “perfect” generally obey several design laws.

## 1. They do not put everything in one interface

Consider the difference:

**Discord**

Server → category → channel → thread → voice → activity → app

**Slack**

Workspace → channel → conversation → canvas → workflow

**Figma**

File → canvas → object → comment → prototype → presentation

**Notion**

Workspace → page → database → block → comment

**Twitch**

Channel → stream → chat → clip → community

**Nextdoor**

Neighbourhood → local information → alert → recommendation → event

The system has **layers**.

That is the secret.

A weak application says:

> “Here are 74 features.”

A strong application says:

> “Here is where you are. Here is what belongs here. Here is what you can do next.”

---

# 2. The strongest products give every object a job

Look at Discord.

A server isn't merely a folder.

A channel isn't merely a chat room.

A role isn't merely a badge.

An Activity isn't merely a mini-game.

A forum isn't merely another message feed.

Each object has a **semantic purpose**.

Likewise in Slack:

Message → conversation
Canvas → knowledge
List → action
Huddle → synchronous discussion
Workflow → automation

This is why it does not feel chaotic despite enormous feature depth.

---

# 3. They use progressive disclosure

This may be the single most important lesson for the product you are designing.

A sophisticated platform should **hide complexity until the complexity becomes relevant**.

Figma does not show you 200 collaboration controls when you first open a design.

Discord does not display every bot action permanently.

Slack does not turn every channel into an enormous dashboard.

Typeform does not show every possible branch simultaneously.

The interface says:

> **Here is the next meaningful thing.**

Then reveals more only when required.

---

# 4. They turn conversation into an object

This happens repeatedly:

Slack:
**message → canvas/workflow**

Teams:
**message → Loop → task**

Chanty:
**message → task**

ClickUp:
**message/idea → task/doc/whiteboard**

Intercom:
**conversation → ticket**

Asana:
**comment/update → task**

Front:
**conversation → assignment/internal collaboration**

Figma:
**comment → design revision**

This is a gigantic product principle.

### The winning question is not:

> “What else can users say?”

It is:

> **“What can users create from what they just said?”**

---

# 5. They distinguish temporary from permanent information

Zulip understands this exceptionally well.

Signal understands it through disappearing messages.

Basecamp separates chat from durable message boards.

Intercom separates conversations from tickets.

Notion treats pages as durable memory.

Discord uses channels/forums for different lifetimes of information.

This matters because information has different temporal values:

**momentary → conversational → actionable → reference → archival**

If everything follows one chronological feed, the system becomes a mess.

---

# 6. They design around a primary human activity

This is why:

**Twitch** feels like a broadcast venue.

**Rec Room** feels like a game.

**Figma** feels like a studio.

**Miro** feels like a workshop.

**Nextdoor** feels like a neighbourhood.

**BAND** feels like a club organiser.

**Jostle** feels like an organisation.

**Discord** feels like a community.

**Slack** feels like an office.

**WhatsApp** feels like a universal telephone book.

The interface communicates the product's **purpose before you read the documentation**.

That is extraordinarily difficult to achieve—and tremendously valuable.

---

And there is a deeper conclusion hiding underneath the entire benchmark:

## The future “chat app” is probably not a chat app.

The trajectory of these products is remarkably consistent:

**Chat**
↓
**Community**
↓
**Content**
↓
**Presence**
↓
**Files / knowledge**
↓
**Tasks / workflows**
↓
**Automation**
↓
**AI agents**
↓
**Transactions / services**
↓
**Persistent digital environments**

Slack's Canvas, ClickUp Whiteboards, Miro AI Workflows, Asana AI Teammates, Zoom AI Companion, Telegram Mini Apps and Status's wallet/community architecture all point towards the same fundamental evolution: **communication becomes the connective tissue between activities rather than the activity itself**. ([Slack][5])

That is also why the most intelligent interfaces feel calm despite enormous functionality.

They do **not** show the user everything.

They build a hierarchy of meaning:

> **Where am I? → Who am I with? → What are we doing? → What matters now? → What can happen next?**

That is the real secret behind the platforms on your list that feel “decorated perfectly” rather than scribbled together.
