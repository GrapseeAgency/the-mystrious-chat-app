     1→I went through the list as a **product-design and systems benchmark**, not merely as a catalogue of messaging features. The crucial question is not “what can users type here?” but:
     2→
     3→> **What does this product turn conversation into?**
     4→
     5→That distinction explains why Discord feels like a community operating system, Slack feels like a work operating system, Twitch feels like a live-media venue, Rec Room feels like a social game, Nextdoor feels like a neighbourhood network, and Figma feels like a design room rather than a chat application.
     6→
     7→One important correction before the benchmark: your 100 names are **not actually 100 independent active chat platforms** in September 2026. Skype was retired on **5 May 2025**; Guilded was sunset at the end of 2025; Meta Workplace closed in May 2026; Amino shut down in December 2025. X Spaces is a feature of X, RCS is a messaging protocol, `bere.al` is associated with BeReal rather than being a separate network, Status Network is an Ethereum L2 rather than a messenger, and “Status IM”, “Session Messaging”, and “Twitter Spaces” overlap with other entries. ([Microsoft Support][1])
     8→
     9→So I will **preserve all 100 positions**, but I will explicitly identify duplicates, protocols, dead products and uncertain names instead of inventing features.

    13→# 1–20 — The major communication architectures
    14→
    15→### 1. Discord — **the community operating system**
    16→
    17→**Beyond chat:** Discord turns one conversation into a persistent world: servers contain categories, text channels, forum channels, voice channels, stages, roles, events and apps. The most important conceptual move is that **a community itself is the product**, not merely the messages inside it.
    18→
    19→**What is unusually intelligent:** Apps can operate inside DMs, group chats, servers and voice calls; Activities allow games, quizzes and collaborative experiences to open directly inside the conversation. Developers can also expose commands through the launcher, slash commands or contextual actions on users/messages. ([Discord Apps][2])
    20→
    21→**Design lesson:** Discord does not dump every capability into the composer. It distributes capabilities according to **context**: talk in a channel, gather in voice, organise through roles, perform actions through apps, play through Activities. That separation is one of its greatest UX achievements.
    22→
    23→**Why it feels polished:** persistent server navigation + hierarchy + recognisable icons + restrained typography + immediate presence indicators make the interface feel like **a place you inhabit**, rather than a pile of messages.
    24→
    25→---
    26→
    27→### 2. Slack — **conversation → work**
    28→
    29→Slack's killer idea is that conversation is only the front door. Channels organise people around projects or subjects, while Huddles, Canvas, Lists, files, workflows and integrations turn discussion into execution. ([Slack][3])
    30→
    31→**The brilliant part:** a Huddle happens directly inside a channel or DM, can include video, multi-person screen sharing, a notes canvas and AI meeting notes. ([Slack][4])
    32→
    33→Canvas is even more important: files, images, video, comments, threads, app data and workflows can live together instead of forcing users to leave Slack. ([Slack][5])
    34→
    35→**Design lesson:** Slack's interface understands a crucial rule:
    36→
    37→**Do not make the user decide which tool to use before they know what they are trying to accomplish.**
    38→
    39→Conversation → canvas → workflow → action.
    40→
    41→That is far more sophisticated than “chat + 50 buttons”.
    42→
    43→---
    44→
    45→### 3. WhatsApp — **messaging as universal infrastructure**
    46→
    47→WhatsApp's strength is not feature extravagance. It is **frictionless ubiquity**.
    48→
    49→The product has gradually expanded from private messaging into Communities, Channels, Status, business messaging, group calling, payments/business interactions in some markets and richer commerce.
    50→
    51→Channels are particularly clever because they deliberately separate **broadcasting from conversation**. A Channel is not pretending to be a group chat: followers receive updates, react and vote without turning the feed into thousands of replies. ([WhatsApp Help Center][6])
    52→
    53→**Design lesson:** WhatsApp demonstrates that the best information architecture often comes from asking:
    54→
    55→> “Should this content be conversational, broadcast, private or transactional?”
    56→
    57→Rather than treating everything as messages.
    58→
    59→---
    60→
    61→### 4. Telegram — **messenger → programmable ecosystem**
    62→
    63→Telegram's defining leap is the **bot + Mini App architecture**. Developers can make bots perform practical tasks and Mini Apps can bring interactive services directly into Telegram; Mini Apps can generate media/files and even integrate with device-level shortcuts. ([Telegram][7])
    64→
    65→Groups and channels can scale dramatically, while topics provide structured discussion inside communities.
    66→
    67→**What makes it beyond chat:** the user can encounter a service without installing another application.
    68→
    69→A payment flow, game, utility, store, form, bot, community or media experience can begin from a message.
    70→
    71→**Design lesson:** Telegram shows the power of treating messages as **launch surfaces for applications**.
    72→
    73→---
    74→
    75→### 5. Signal — **private communication as a complete environment**
    76→
    77→Signal has gone significantly beyond “encrypted SMS”. Current features include polls, pinned messages, group member labels, usernames/phone-number privacy, Stories, scheduled messages, screen sharing, chat folders, disappearing messages, themes and Note to Self. ([Signal Support][8])
    78→
    79→The beautifully understated feature is **Note to Self**: your private messenger also becomes an encrypted personal notebook/file bridge. ([Signal Support][9])
    80→
    81→Stories retain Signal's privacy philosophy rather than becoming an advertising feed; you control who can see them, including custom stories and group stories. ([Signal Support][10])
    82→
    83→**Design lesson:** Signal's sophistication is mostly invisible. Its UX treats privacy as a **default interaction model**, not a settings page.
    84→
    85→---
    86→
    87→### 6. Element — **communication sovereignty**
    88→
    89→Element is interesting because it is not merely a prettier secure messenger. It sits on Matrix and combines messaging, spaces, files, voice/video, integrations, widgets and self-hosting/decentralisation. ([Element][11])
    90→
    91→Spaces group rooms into larger communities; Matrix itself can bridge communities across systems such as IRC, Discord and Slack. ([matrix.org][12])
    92→
    93→**Beyond chat:** the deeper product is **ownership of communications infrastructure**.
    94→
    95→**Design lesson:** Element demonstrates that “UX” can extend all the way down to architecture: a user can perceive decentralisation through spaces, homeservers, verification and interoperability rather than having to understand the protocol.
    96→
    97→---
    98→
    99→### 7. Revolt — **Discord-like community architecture with openness**
   100→
   101→Revolt's appeal is its community-server architecture: servers, channels, roles, permissions and a Discord-like social structure, but with an open-source/community-oriented positioning.
   102→
   103→**Beyond chat:** the meaningful differentiator is not a single killer feature; it is the ability to construct **your own communication environment** rather than merely participate in somebody else's feed.
   104→
   105→**Design lesson:** Revolt reinforces how powerful Discord's information architecture is: server → categories → channels → permissions. The hierarchy creates mental order before conversation begins.
   106→
   107→---
   108→
   109→### 8. Beeper — **the inbox abstraction**
   110→
   111→Beeper's innovation is conceptually enormous: it attempts to make many networks feel like **one messaging inbox**. Current Beeper connects services including WhatsApp, Instagram, Telegram, Signal, Messenger, X, Google Messages, Google Chat, LinkedIn, Discord and Slack. ([Beeper][13])
   112→
   113→Its newer architecture emphasises on-device connections for many services, with cross-device synchronisation and productivity features such as reminders and scheduling. ([Beeper][13])
   114→
   115→**Beyond chat:** Beeper's product is actually **information consolidation**.
   116→
   117→**Design lesson:** it attacks one of modern communication's biggest problems: users do not have too few chats; they have **too many inboxes**.
   118→
   119→---
   120→
   121→### 9. Threema — **privacy-first communication with practical structure**
   122→
   123→Threema's philosophy is closer to “secure communication toolkit” than social network. It combines messaging with encrypted calls, groups, file sharing, polls and organisational functionality.
   124→
   125→**Beyond chat:** the differentiator is the attempt to make privacy compatible with normal daily collaboration rather than forcing the user into a minimalist cryptographic tool.
   126→
   127→**Design lesson:** strong privacy UX works best when security mechanisms are integrated into ordinary interactions rather than presented as technical ceremony.
   128→
   129→---
   130→
   131→### 10. Viber — **messaging + expressive social layer + communities**
   132→
   133→Viber combines private and group chats, voice/video calls, communities, stickers/GIFs, message editing/deletion, disappearing messages and synchronisation across devices. It also offers calls to ordinary phone numbers through Viber Out. ([Viber][14])
   134→
   135→**Beyond chat:** Viber treats **expression** as a product category. Stickers, GIFs, reactions, custom content and communities are not afterthoughts.
   136→
   137→**Design lesson:** communication is not only semantic information. It is emotion, identity, play and social signalling. Viber makes those layers visually available without replacing the basic message flow.
   138→
   139→---
   140→
   141→### 11. WeChat — **the super-app benchmark**
   142→
   143→WeChat's real significance is what happens when messaging becomes a **gateway to an ecosystem**: social feeds, mini-programs, commerce, payments, services, official accounts and numerous everyday functions.
   144→
   145→**Beyond chat:** the user does not think, “Now I am leaving messaging to use a service.” Messaging becomes the navigation layer for daily life.
   146→
   147→**Design lesson:** the super-app model succeeds when the platform turns **identity + communication + transaction + service discovery** into one continuous experience.
   148→
   149→---
   150→
   151→### 12. LINE — **messenger as entertainment culture**
   152→
   153→LINE combines conventional messaging with stickers, communities, social content, services, games and a deep sticker/character ecosystem.
   154→
   155→**The clever part:** LINE has historically treated stickers as a **visual language**, not merely decoration.
   156→
   157→A sticker communicates tone that text struggles to express: affection, sarcasm, celebration, embarrassment, frustration, etc.
   158→
   159→**Design lesson:** a platform becomes culturally sticky when the UI acquires its own **native grammar of expression**.
   160→
   161→---
   162→
   163→### 13. KakaoTalk — **messaging + services + identity**
   164→
   165→KakaoTalk is deeply integrated into the broader Kakao ecosystem: open chats, channels, commerce, gifts, business services and other products.
   166→
   167→**Beyond chat:** communication becomes a gateway into an **identity-and-services ecosystem**.
   168→
   169→**Design lesson:** “one app” works when adjacent capabilities share the same identity, contact graph and interaction vocabulary.
   170→
   171→---
   172→
   173→### 14. Session — **messaging without conventional identity assumptions**
   174→
   175→Session is notable for privacy architecture based on decentralised communication and metadata protection, rather than simply applying encryption to a conventional phone-number messenger.
   176→
   177→**Beyond chat:** the important product idea is **anonymous/pseudonymous identity**, where the user is not forced to expose a normal social identifier.
   178→
   179→**Design lesson:** identity itself is a product decision. A messaging system can ask “Who are you?” very differently from WhatsApp or Signal.
   180→
   181→---
   182→
   183→### 15. Wickr — **secure organisational communication**
   184→
   185→Wickr's legacy is particularly relevant to enterprise messaging: secure messaging, control, administration, retention and organisational deployment.
   186→
   187→However, the old Wickr Pro product was discontinued and AWS consolidated the product line into AWS Wickr; AWS documentation confirms the migration/discontinuation path. ([Amazon Web Services][15])
   188→
   189→**Beyond chat:** organisational policy becomes part of the communication product.
   190→
   191→**Design lesson:** enterprise communication is not merely “consumer chat with more users”. Auditability, identity, administration and governance are first-class UX.
   192→
   193→---
   194→
   195→### 16. Matrix — **not a chat app; an entire communication protocol**
   196→
   197→Matrix should be treated separately from Element.
   198→
   199→Matrix is an open standard/ecosystem with servers, clients, bridges, bots, SDKs and decentralised communication. ([matrix.org][16])
   200→
   201→Its most extraordinary property is federation: people on different servers can still communicate.
   202→
   203→Bridges can connect Matrix environments with other communications systems such as Slack, Discord and IRC. ([matrix.org][17])
   204→
   205→**Beyond chat:** Matrix is essentially an **internet protocol for conversations**.
   206→
   207→**Design lesson:** the deepest level of product design is sometimes not an interface at all—it is the architecture that allows multiple interfaces to exist.
   208→
   209→---
   210→
   211→### 17. Twitch — **conversation attached to live media**
   212→
   213→Twitch does not make chat the centre of gravity. **The stream is the event; chat is the audience layer.**
   214→
   215→Subscriptions, Bits, emotes, raids, clips, moderation, channel points and live interaction transform viewers from passive spectators into participants.
   216→
   217→**Why the interface works:** the stream occupies the dominant visual area; chat remains persistent but subordinate. This hierarchy mirrors what the user came to do.
   218→
   219→**Design lesson:** UI hierarchy should reflect **the user's primary activity**, not the number of available features.
   220→
   221→---
   222→
   223→### 18. Microsoft Teams — **communication fused with the Microsoft work graph**
   224→
   225→Teams brings chats and channels together with meetings, Loop components, files, Microsoft 365 content and third-party apps. ([Microsoft][18])
   226→
   227→Collaborative notes can exist directly inside chats, with agendas, tasks and real-time editing; assigned tasks can flow into To Do/Planner. ([Microsoft Support][19])
   228→
   229→**Beyond chat:** Teams turns communication into **work objects**.
   230→
   231→**Design lesson:** the most powerful integration is not a link-out. It is when an object remains **alive inside the conversation**.
   232→
   233→---
   234→
   235→### 19. Google Chat — **conversation inside Google's information ecosystem**
   236→
   237→Google Chat is strongest when it inherits the surrounding Google Workspace model: spaces, threaded conversations, files, Drive, Calendar, Meet, apps and collaborative documents.
   238→
   239→**Beyond chat:** a message can be the doorway to a broader Workspace action.
   240→
   241→**Design lesson:** Google's advantage is contextual continuity. The user already has identity, documents, calendar and organisational data, so communication becomes another layer over the same graph.
   242→
   243→---
   244→
   245→### 20. Zoom — **meeting platform evolving into work platform**
   246→
   247→Zoom has been expanding from meetings into Team Chat, Docs, Whiteboards, Clips, AI Companion and Workspace-style information organisation.
   248→
   249→The current AI chat panel can work from Zoom content such as meetings, chats and docs, while also accepting external data sources such as Google Drive and Microsoft 365. ([Zoom][20])
   250→
   251→Zoom can also generate documents, data tables, whiteboards and clips through AI Companion. ([Zoom][21])
   252→
   253→**Beyond chat:** Zoom is attempting to make **communication itself the source material for work**.
   254→
   255→---

   257→# 21–40 — collaboration, gaming and live communication
   258→
   259→### 21. Webex — **meeting → workspace**
   260→
   261→Webex organises work around Spaces and Teams where users can message, call, meet, share files and use whiteboards. ([Webex Help Center][22])
   262→
   263→Its strength is continuity: a space remains after the meeting ends.
   264→
   265→**Design lesson:** meeting software becomes much more useful when the room does not disappear after everyone hangs up.
   266→
   267→---
   268→
   269→### 22. Chanty — **chat with an action layer**
   270→
   271→Chanty deliberately embeds tasks into communication: messages can become tasks, tasks have status/assignees, and task discussions remain contextual. It also offers Kanban, Teambook, announcements, calendars, polls, forms, calls and knowledge organisation. ([Chanty][23])
   272→
   273→**Why the design is intelligent:** it does not make the user “copy the message into a project-management system”.
   274→
   275→**The message itself becomes the work item.**
   276→
   277→---
   278→
   279→### 23. Flock — **team hub**
   280→
   281→Flock combines channels and DMs with video calls, file discovery, app integrations, directories, announcements, mailing lists and bookmarks. Its interface explicitly positions itself as a place where users can perform several work actions without changing tabs. ([Flock][24])
   282→
   283→**Design lesson:** one of the most underappreciated UX problems is **context switching**.
   284→
   285→Flock attacks it directly.
   286→
   287→---
   288→
   289→### 24. Ryver — **forums + groups + direct communication**
   290→
   291→Ryver separates public forums, private group conversations and person-to-person communication. ([Ryver][25])
   292→
   293→**Beyond chat:** the useful distinction is between **broadcast/open discussion** and **private working conversation**.
   294→
   295→**Design lesson:** conversation topology matters as much as messaging technology.
   296→
   297→---
   298→
   299→### 25. Mattermost — **self-hosted operational communication**
   300→
   301→Mattermost is designed around channels, teams, discussions and direct messages, while extending into operational workflows, integrations and self-hosting.
   302→
   303→**Beyond chat:** the product is particularly relevant to organisations that consider communications infrastructure part of operational control.
   304→
   305→**Design lesson:** enterprise UX can be excellent without copying consumer platforms; **control and auditability can themselves be design features**.
   306→
   307→---
   308→
   309→### 26. Rocket.Chat — **chat + omnichannel service desk**
   310→
   311→Rocket.Chat combines team collaboration with an omnichannel customer-engagement layer. Its documentation explicitly covers channels, teams, discussions, DMs, threads, file sharing, search, Livechat, email, WhatsApp and other channel integrations. ([Rocket.Chat Documentation][26])
   312→
   313→**Beyond chat:** the same agent can handle an internal discussion and an external customer interaction.
   314→
   315→**Design lesson:** the real object is not “message”; it is **conversation across channels**.
   316→
   317→---
   318→
   319→### 27. Zulip — **the topic architecture is the feature**
   320→
   321→Zulip's defining innovation is topic-based conversation.
   322→
   323→Several conversations can exist inside one channel without becoming one giant chronological river. Topics remain readable days later, and messages are automatically associated with the conversation being viewed. ([Zulip][27])
   324→
   325→Zulip additionally has collaborative messages, polls, to-do lists, reminders, integrations, bots and even bridge functionality. ([Zulip][28])
   326→
   327→**Why it is unusually intelligent:** it solves the fundamental weakness of traditional chat:
   328→
   329→**time is a terrible information architecture.**
   330→
   331→Topics turn chat from a river into an indexed knowledge system.
   332→

   347→### 29. Guilded — **historically superb gaming-community architecture, now closed**
   348→
   349→Guilded was particularly interesting because it went beyond Discord-style messaging into gaming-specific structures: calendars, scheduling, forums, docs, voice, recruitment-style community organisation and server tooling.
   350→
   351→But it is no longer an active platform: Roblox announced that Guilded would sunset at the end of 2025. ([DevForum][29])
   352→
   353→**Design lesson from the product that existed:** community software becomes substantially more useful when it understands **what the community actually does**.
   354→
   355→For gaming, that means events, teams, games, schedules and roles—not merely channels.
   356→

   358→
   359→### 30. TeamSpeak — **voice architecture as the product**
   360→
   361→TeamSpeak has historically concentrated on highly controllable voice servers, channels, permissions and low-level administration.
   362→
   363→**Beyond chat:** it treats voice as infrastructure rather than simply “another message type”.
   364→
   365→**Design lesson:** for some communities, the most important UX is not text—it is **presence, voice hierarchy, permissions and stability**.
   366→
   367→---
   368→
   369→### 31. Mumble — **low-latency positional communication**
   370→
   371→Mumble's defining characteristic is high-quality low-latency voice, including positional audio in supported environments.
   372→
   373→**Beyond chat:** users can perceive *where* someone is in relation to them in a virtual environment.
   374→
   375→That is a profound shift from:
   376→
   377→> “Who sent this message?”
   378→
   379→to:
   380→
   381→> “Where is that person in this space?”
   382→
   383→---
   384→
   385→### 32. Steam Chat — **social layer attached to gaming identity**
   386→
   387→Steam Chat connects directly with game presence: friends can be grouped by game, favourites can be surfaced, group chats persist, and rich presence can show what friends are doing inside games. ([Steam Community][30])
   388→
   389→**Beyond chat:** the system knows **what activity generated the social interaction**.
   390→
   391→**Design lesson:** presence becomes more valuable when it represents something meaningful: “playing X”, “in a match”, “joinable”, rather than merely “online”.
   392→
   393→---
   394→
   395→### 33. Skype — **historic communication benchmark, no longer active**
   396→
   397→Skype once revolutionised internet calling, screen sharing, group video, messaging and international calling.
   398→
   399→But it was retired on **5 May 2025**, with Microsoft directing users towards Teams Free. ([Microsoft Support][1])
   400→
   401→**Design lesson:** being historically dominant does not mean the information architecture remains optimal. Platforms can be displaced when a newer product absorbs their strongest capabilities into a broader ecosystem.
   402→
   403→---
   404→
   405→### 34. VRChat — **communication as inhabitable 3D space**
   406→
   407→VRChat is radically different because identity is represented through avatars and conversation happens while users inhabit worlds.
   408→
   409→**Beyond chat:** identity, body language, distance, spatial audio, gestures, world design and community events all become communication.
   410→
   411→**Design lesson:** the strongest social interfaces sometimes remove the conventional UI almost entirely and let **the environment become the interface**.
   412→
   413→---
   414→
   415→### 35. Rec Room — **social network disguised as a game platform**
   416→
   417→Rec Room describes itself as a social app played like a game, with millions of player-created rooms and a Maker Pen for constructing interactive experiences. ([Rec Room][31])
   418→
   419→**Beyond chat:** the user can **build the thing they are talking about**.
   420→
   421→That is an extraordinarily strong community loop:
   422→
   423→Create → share → visit → play → discuss → remix.
   424→
   425→---
   426→
   427→### 36. Snapchat — **camera-first social communication**
   428→
   429→Snapchat starts with the camera rather than the keyboard. Stories, Spotlight, Chat, Lenses and Maps create a system in which communication, entertainment and location-based discovery coexist. ([Snapchat][32])
   430→
   431→**Why the design is exceptional:** each function has its own visual behaviour.
   432→
   433→Lenses = transformation.
   434→Stories = temporary narrative.
   435→Chat = private conversation.
   436→Spotlight = discovery.
   437→Map = spatial awareness.
   438→
   439→It avoids the mistake of making all content look like identical posts.
   440→

   442→
   443→### 37. Instagram DMs — **private communication inside a visual identity network**
   444→
   445→Instagram DMs inherit the platform's enormous media graph: profiles, posts, Reels, Stories and creators.
   446→
   447→**Beyond chat:** sharing is contextual. You rarely send a naked URL—you send the Instagram object itself.
   448→
   449→**Design lesson:** communication becomes richer when the network's **content objects are native to the conversation**.
   450→
   451→---
   452→
   453→### 38. TikTok Inbox — **social discovery feeding conversation**
   454→
   455→TikTok's messaging is inseparable from video discovery.
   456→
   457→The user can discover a piece of content, react to it, share it privately and bring other people into the context.
   458→
   459→**Beyond chat:** the primary social object is **media**, while messaging is the connective tissue around it.
   460→
   461→This reverses the classic messenger architecture.
   462→
   463→---
   464→
   465→### 39. X DMs — **private messaging attached to a public discourse network**
   466→
   467→X DMs are interesting precisely because they exist beside public posting, Communities and Spaces.
   468→
   469→**Beyond chat:** users can move between:
   470→
   471→public statement → public conversation → live audio → private conversation.
   472→
   473→X therefore treats messaging as one layer within a broader **public/private communication continuum**. X describes Communities, Spaces, DMs, long-form posts, video and subscription mechanisms as parts of one ecosystem. ([Help Center][33])
   474→
   475→---
   476→
   477→### 40. Threads — **conversation-first social network**
   478→
   479→Threads is principally a public social-discussion environment rather than a traditional messenger.
   480→
   481→Its relevant lesson is the distinction between **public conversation and private messaging**: the two have different incentives, visibility and interface needs.
   482→
   483→**Design lesson:** never assume that a private DM UI can simply be scaled into a public community UI. The psychology changes.

   487→# 41–60 — social identity, ephemeral communication and alternative protocols
   488→
   489→### 41. iMessage — **communication as OS-level magic**
   490→
   491→iMessage is powerful because communication is deeply integrated into Apple's ecosystem.
   492→
   493→Features such as reactions, rich media, effects, shared experiences and safety/location-oriented functionality make the message thread feel like an interactive space rather than SMS.
   494→
   495→**Design lesson:** platform-native communication feels luxurious when the underlying operating system removes friction.
   496→
   497→The user does not feel like they are “opening another service”.
   498→
   499→---
   500→
   501→### 42. Google Messages — **RCS plus increasingly expressive messaging**
   502→
   503→Google Messages combines SMS/MMS fallback with RCS capabilities such as typing indicators, read receipts, high-resolution media and richer group communication. ([Google Help][34])
   504→
   505→Its current design experimentation also includes Screen Effects and custom conversation bubbles that let individual chats acquire distinct visual identities. ([Google Help][35])
   506→
   507→**Design lesson:** personalisation can be valuable when it is **local to the relationship** rather than an endless global theme store.
   508→
   509→---
   510→
   511→### 43. RCS Chat — **not an app**
   512→
   513→RCS is a communication protocol.
   514→
   515→It provides capabilities such as typing indicators, read receipts, Wi-Fi/data messaging and high-resolution file/media transfer. ([Google Help][34])
   516→
   517→The important “beyond chat” lesson is architectural:
   518→
   519→RCS attempts to make **carrier messaging behave like modern internet messaging**.
   520→
   521→It is therefore a protocol-level benchmark rather than a UI benchmark.
   522→
   523→---
   524→
   525→### 44. Voxer — **voice messaging as walkie-talkie communication**
   526→
   527→Voxer is built around push-to-talk/live voice combined with conventional text and media.
   528→
   529→**Beyond chat:** voice is neither a phone call nor a recorded voicemail; it sits in the middle as **asynchronous radio**.
   530→
   531→**Design lesson:** communication modes do not need to be binary (“text” vs “call”). There is an enormous design space between them.
   532→
   533→---
   534→
   535→### 45. Zello — **professional push-to-talk**
   536→
   537→Zello applies the walkie-talkie model to smartphones, with channels, push-to-talk communication and location-aware/team-oriented scenarios.
   538→
   539→**Beyond chat:** the interaction is designed around **fast coordination**, not leisurely conversation.
   540→
   541→**Design lesson:** optimise the UI around the temporal needs of the activity.

  1193→### 99. MeWe — **social network without algorithmic dependency**
  1194→
  1195→MeWe offers feeds, pages, groups, private chats, stories, disappearing content, voice/video, cloud storage, dual-camera functionality and group-specific profiles. ([MeWe Support][67])
  1196→
  1197→Its Groups provide dedicated chat, shared storage, events, smart search and tagging. ([MeWe Support][68])
  1198→
  1199→Its distinctive philosophy is chronological/user-controlled feeds rather than aggressive algorithmic filtering. ([MeWe Support][69])
  1200→
  1201→**Beyond chat:** MeWe attempts to make **ownership and control** part of the social experience.
  1202→

  1205→### 100. Nextdoor — **neighbourhood intelligence**
  1206→
  1207→Nextdoor may be one of the most conceptually different products in your list.
  1208→
  1209→It combines neighbourhood identity with local news, safety alerts, recommendations, listings, events and local businesses. ([Nextdoor][70])
  1210→
  1211→Its AI-powered Faves system can synthesise years of neighbourhood discussions to answer practical local questions. ([Nextdoor Blog][71])

  1214→
  1215→It is:
  1216→
  1217→**local knowledge + trust + place + recommendations + safety + commerce + community.**
  1218→
  1219→That is why its product model is so interesting.

  1222→
  1223→# Now the really important part: **why do the best ones not look like scribble?**
  1224→
  1225→This is where the 100-platform comparison becomes genuinely useful.
  1226→
  1227→The products that feel “perfect” generally obey several design laws.
  1228→
  1229→## 1. They do not put everything in one interface
  1230→
  1231→Consider the difference:
  1232→
  1233→**Discord**
  1234→
  1235→Server → category → channel → thread → voice → activity → app
  1236→
  1237→**Slack**
  1238→
  1239→Workspace → channel → conversation → canvas → workflow
  1240→
  1241→**Figma**
  1242→
  1243→File → canvas → object → comment → prototype → presentation
  1244→
  1245→**Notion**
  1246→
  1247→Workspace → page → database → block → comment
  1248→
  1249→**Twitch**
  1250→
  1251→Channel → stream → chat → clip → community
  1252→
  1253→**Nextdoor**
  1254→
  1255→Neighbourhood → local information → alert → recommendation → event
  1256→
  1257→The system has **layers**.
  1258→
  1259→That is the secret.
  1260→
  1261→A weak application says:
  1262→
  1263→> “Here are 74 features.”
  1264→
  1265→A strong application says:
  1266→
  1267→> “Here is where you are. Here is what belongs here. Here is what you can do next.”
  1268→
  1269→---
  1270→
  1271→# 2. The strongest products give every object a job
  1272→
  1273→Look at Discord.
  1274→
  1275→A server isn't merely a folder.
  1276→
  1277→A channel isn't merely a chat room.
  1278→
  1279→A role isn't merely a badge.
  1280→
  1281→An Activity isn't merely a mini-game.
  1282→
  1283→A forum isn't merely another message feed.
  1284→
  1285→Each object has a **semantic purpose**.
  1286→
  1287→Likewise in Slack:
  1288→
  1289→Message → conversation
  1290→Canvas → knowledge
  1291→List → action
  1292→Huddle → synchronous discussion
  1293→Workflow → automation
  1294→
  1295→This is why it does not feel chaotic despite enormous feature depth.
  1296→
  1297→---
  1298→
  1299→# 3. They use progressive disclosure
  1300→
  1301→This may be the single most important lesson for the product you are designing.
  1302→
  1303→A sophisticated platform should **hide complexity until the complexity becomes relevant**.
  1304→
  1305→Figma does not show you 200 collaboration controls when you first open a design.
  1306→
  1307→Discord does not display every bot action permanently.
  1308→
  1309→Slack does not turn every channel into an enormous dashboard.
  1310→
  1311→Typeform does not show every possible branch simultaneously.
  1312→
  1313→The interface says:
  1314→
  1315→> **Here is the next meaningful thing.**
  1316→
  1317→Then reveals more only when required.
  1318→
  1319→---
  1320→
  1321→# 4. They turn conversation into an object
  1322→
  1323→This happens repeatedly:
  1324→
  1325→Slack:
  1326→**message → canvas/workflow**
  1327→
  1328→Teams:
  1329→**message → Loop → task**
  1330→
  1331→Chanty:
  1332→**message → task**
  1333→
  1334→ClickUp:
  1335→**message/idea → task/doc/whiteboard**
  1336→
  1337→Intercom:
  1338→**conversation → ticket**
  1339→
  1340→Asana:
  1341→**comment/update → task**
  1342→
  1343→Front:
  1344→**conversation → assignment/internal collaboration**
  1345→
  1346→Figma:
  1347→**comment → design revision**
  1348→
  1349→This is a gigantic product principle.
  1350→
  1351→### The winning question is not:
  1352→
  1353→> “What else can users say?”
  1354→
  1355→It is:
  1356→
  1357→> **“What can users create from what they just said?”**
  1358→
  1359→---
  1360→
  1361→# 5. They distinguish temporary from permanent information
  1362→
  1363→Zulip understands this exceptionally well.
  1364→
  1365→Signal understands it through disappearing messages.
  1366→
  1367→Basecamp separates chat from durable message boards.
  1368→
  1369→Intercom separates conversations from tickets.
  1370→
  1371→Notion treats pages as durable memory.
  1372→
  1373→Discord uses channels/forums for different lifetimes of information.
  1374→
  1375→This matters because information has different temporal values:
  1376→
  1377→**momentary → conversational → actionable → reference → archival**
  1378→
  1379→If everything follows one chronological feed, the system becomes a mess.
  1380→
  1381→---
  1382→
  1383→# 6. They design around a primary human activity
  1384→
  1385→This is why:
  1386→
  1387→**Twitch** feels like a broadcast venue.
  1388→
  1389→**Rec Room** feels like a game.
  1390→
  1391→**Figma** feels like a studio.
  1392→
  1393→**Miro** feels like a workshop.
  1394→
  1395→**Nextdoor** feels like a neighbourhood.
  1396→
  1397→**BAND** feels like a club organiser.
  1398→
  1399→**Jostle** feels like an organisation.
  1400→
  1401→**Discord** feels like a community.
  1402→
  1403→**Slack** feels like an office.
  1404→
  1405→**WhatsApp** feels like a universal telephone book.
  1406→
  1407→The interface communicates the product's **purpose before you read the documentation**.
  1408→
  1409→That is extraordinarily difficult to achieve—and tremendously valuable.
  1410→

  1447→And there is a deeper conclusion hiding underneath the entire benchmark:
  1448→
  1449→## The future “chat app” is probably not a chat app.
  1450→
  1451→The trajectory of these products is remarkably consistent:
  1452→
  1453→**Chat**
  1454→↓
  1455→**Community**
  1456→↓
  1457→**Content**
  1458→↓
  1459→**Presence**
  1460→↓
  1461→**Files / knowledge**
  1462→↓
  1463→**Tasks / workflows**
  1464→↓
  1465→**Automation**
  1466→↓
  1467→**AI agents**
  1468→↓
  1469→**Transactions / services**
  1470→↓
  1471→**Persistent digital environments**
  1472→
  1473→Slack's Canvas, ClickUp Whiteboards, Miro AI Workflows, Asana AI Teammates, Zoom AI Companion, Telegram Mini Apps and Status's wallet/community architecture all point towards the same fundamental evolution: **communication becomes the connective tissue between activities rather than the activity itself**. ([Slack][5])
  1474→
  1475→That is also why the most intelligent interfaces feel calm despite enormous functionality.
  1476→
  1477→They do **not** show the user everything.
  1478→
  1479→They build a hierarchy of meaning:
  1480→
  1481→> **Where am I? → Who am I with? → What are we doing? → What matters now? → What can happen next?**
  1482→
  1483→That is the real secret behind the platforms on your list that feel “decorated perfectly” rather than scribbled together.
